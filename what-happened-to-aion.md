Aion launched in 2017, shipped mainnet in 2018, renamed its public-facing network around The OAN in 2019, closed its ERC-20 token swap in 2022, and continues to run a mainnet with maintained node software and a desktop wallet.

That is the factual sequence. Most of the confusion around the project comes from reading one of those events as an ending, when each was a change within a project that kept operating.

## You'll see this if

- You found a 2018 article and a 2020 one that describe different names.
- You are trying to work out whether the project shut down.
- You hold an old ERC-20 balance and want to know why it cannot be converted.
- You are piecing together the timeline from forum posts.

## The timeline, without speculation

2017: the project is founded in Toronto and raises funding for an interoperability-focused chain, built around a Java virtual machine rather than the EVM.

2018: mainnet launches, and an ERC-20 token on Ethereum is swapped for native AION through a bridge that was one of the earliest production examples of its kind.

2019: the network is rebranded as The Open Application Network, with AION kept as the asset name. The technology did not change; the positioning and the public name did.

2022: the swap window closes. After 31 January 2022 an ERC-20 balance can no longer be converted through the official process, which is why the [Aion token swap closed](https://github.com/aion-wallet/aion-explained/blob/main/aion-token-swap-closed.md) article exists.

Since then: node releases continue, including consensus-relevant updates, and the desktop wallet is maintained with installers for three platforms.

## Why the history reads as a decline

Three things create that impression.

The rebrand split search results. Material before 2019 says Aion, material after says The OAN, and a reader who finds only one half concludes the project changed identity or disappeared.

The ecosystem contracted. Wallets and services that supported AION in 2018 have closed or dropped the network, so old guides link to things that no longer exist. The [Aion crypto wallet choices](https://github.com/aion-wallet/aion-wallet-help/blob/main/aion-crypto-wallet-choices.md) article lists what actually works now.

Market attention moved elsewhere. That is a fact about sentiment rather than about the chain, and it belongs in a market discussion rather than in a technical history.

## What did not happen

The chain was not shut down, the token was not migrated to a new network, and the code was not abandoned. A reader who concludes any of those from a date on a blog post has been misled by the shape of the timeline rather than by its content.

The project also did not become an EVM chain. The AVM remains the execution environment, which is why Ethereum tooling does not apply, as the [Aion Java smart contract](https://github.com/aion-wallet/aion-explained/blob/main/aion-java-smart-contract.md) article explains.

## What it means for a holder today

Hold AION in the current desktop wallet, keep the recovery phrase backed up, and check the release page rather than a news article when you want to know what version exists.

If you hold an ERC-20 balance from 2018, it is a historical artefact: the swap is closed and no official route converts it. Treat any service offering to do so as a scam until proven otherwise by the project itself.

If you hold AION in an old extension or an older desktop build, the coins are recoverable by restoring the same phrase in the current release, covered by the [Aion Aiwa wallet recovery](https://github.com/aion-wallet/aion-wallet-help/blob/main/aion-aiwa-wallet-recovery.md) and migration guides.

## Prevention

Date every source you read about this project. A guide from 2019 is not wrong about 2019; it is simply not about now.

Prefer the release page to commentary. A version and a date settle most questions faster than an argument.

Keep your own record: the address, the backup, the version you installed. History is easier to navigate when your own part of it is written down.

## The parts that are often misreported

Three claims appear repeatedly and are worth correcting whenever you meet them.

The first is that the chain migrated to a new network. It did not: the rebrand changed the public name, not the chain, the addresses or the token. A wallet from before 2019 still opens today with the same address.

The second is that the token was replaced. It was not: the ERC-20 form was a sale artefact that could be converted until 2022, and the native coin is the same asset it always was. The [Aion ERC-20 or mainnet](https://github.com/aion-wallet/aion-explained/blob/main/aion-erc-20-or-mainnet.md) article separates the two forms.

The third is that the project stopped shipping software. The node repository continues to publish releases, including consensus-relevant updates, and the desktop wallet is maintained on this account.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did Aion shut down?

No. The network operates and the software is maintained; the ecosystem around it is smaller than it was.

### Why did the name change?

The network was rebranded as The Open Application Network in 2019, with AION kept as the token name. The [Aion vs the OAN](https://github.com/aion-wallet/aion-explained/blob/main/aion-vs-the-oan.md) article covers it.

### Can I still swap ERC-20 AION?

No. The official swap closed on 31 January 2022.

### Is the current wallet the same software as in 2018?

It is the same project and the same chain, with a client that has been updated since. Install the current release rather than an old build.
